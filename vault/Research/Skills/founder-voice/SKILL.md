# Founder Voice

The voice of someone building something, explaining it plainly to a person who doesn't have the context yet. Composed prose - full sentences, some light structure - but stripped of every move that makes an About page read like it was written by a committee or an AI. Flat, concrete, direct. Big ambition stated in a level voice.

This is **not** a "sound casual" filter. Adding contractions and an exclamation point to generic copy is the opposite of what this does. The work is subtractive: cut the abstractions, cut the hedges, cut the connective tissue, and let concrete claims and the occasional sharp one-liner carry the paragraph.

## Two modes

Figure out which one the task needs before writing.

**Storytelling** - a founder's note, a welcome page, "why this exists", the origin of the project. First person. Opens on the idea or the problem, never on a greeting or a mission-statement throat-clear. Moves through what the person saw, why it bothered them, what they decided to do about it. Closes on what they're hoping for - stated plainly, not inflated.

**Explaining** - making a technical or unfamiliar idea land for a non-expert. Leads with a concrete everyday analogy (a chat thread, a workshop bench, shelves of books) and lets it do the work. Short declaratives. Names the contrast that matters in a handful of words ("exposed to you, not just defined at you") instead of a paragraph of definition.

Both modes share the mechanics below.

## Mechanics

1. **No em-dash.** Never the `—` character. Where a beat wants that pause or aside, use a plain hyphen `-`. This single substitution removes the most common tell of AI-written "casual" prose.
2. **Concrete over abstract.** Kill "empower", "seamless", "cutting-edge", "passionate about", "on a mission to", "every step of the journey", "vibrant community", "lifelong learners". Say the specific thing instead. If a sentence would survive being pasted into any other company's About page, it's too generic - rewrite it.
3. **Short declaratives, and fragments chained with periods for emphasis.** "That is expected." can be its own sentence. A run of three plain statements before the first complex one sets the rhythm.
4. **One aphoristic line per section, doing the load-bearing work.** A short parallel-structured statement that lands the point and gets no follow-up explaining it ("The unfamiliar word is the doorway, not the wall." / "Reuse over recall."). Don't overuse - one per section, not one per paragraph.
5. **Direct second-person address.** Describe the reader by what they do, in parallel clauses: "you drive your own way through, you chase the threads that matter, you don't wait to be walked from one idea to the next." No "such as" or "for instance".
6. **A colon does the work of a subordinate clause.** "The harder thing is turning it into knowledge: something you can examine, connect, and apply." - not "...knowledge, which can be defined as something that...".
7. **Ambition in a level voice.** State the largest claim as a plain fact. "Its mission is to make intelligence accessible." Not "We're on an audacious mission to fundamentally transform...".
8. **Optional: a reflective close.** A final line that reaches slightly past the immediate point - a hope for who this eventually reaches. Some writers close on a bare-name sign-off, or code-switch into another language for that one line. Keep it if it's genuinely the writer's; don't manufacture it.

## Anti-patterns - what to strip

- **Hedging throat-clearers**: "it's important to note", "it's worth considering", "we believe that". If there's a belief, state it as a fact.
- **Enthusiasm markers**: "Great news!", exclamation points for excitement, exclamatory openers. Stay flat even when the content is ambitious.
- **Transitional connective tissue**: "Additionally", "Furthermore", "That said", "Moreover". Let statements sit next to each other.
- **Mission-statement boilerplate**: "curated collection of high-quality resources", "designed to empower", "break down barriers", "foster a community". All of it.
- **Meta-narration**: "In this note, I want to explain...", "To summarize...". Just say the thing.
- **The smoothing instinct.** When you have a blunt, clear sentence, resist rewriting it into something more "professional". Blunt and clear beats smooth and generic every time.

## How to use this

1. Pick the mode - storytelling or explaining.
2. Draft the substance first, plainly.
3. Cut: every abstraction, every hedge, every transition word, every exclamation point, every em-dash.
4. Add back exactly one thing per section - a concrete image, or one sharp line.
5. End when the point lands. No summary paragraph.

## Example transformation

Generic AI draft:

> Our library is a carefully curated collection of high-quality resources designed to empower learners at every stage of their journey. We believe that knowledge should be accessible to everyone, which is why we've made it our mission to break down barriers to education and foster a vibrant community of lifelong learners.

Founder voice:

> This is less an archive than a workspace. It holds frameworks, research, references, and working documents, kept in a way that invites you to take them apart and reuse them rather than only read them.
>
> I wanted it to stay open. Not sitting behind an institution that decides who gets to learn what, and not filtered through a feed built to hold attention. Just the material, organised well enough that a learner, an educator, or someone building on their own can find a way through it.

What changed: "empower", "every stage of their journey", "vibrant community", "lifelong learners" - gone. "I wanted it to stay open." stands alone. The claims are specific enough that they couldn't be pasted onto another project's page.
