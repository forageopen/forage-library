# Founder-voice samples

Four short passages in the voice this skill produces, drawn from a real independent project's public pages and de-personalized (name, project name, and biographical specifics removed or replaced with placeholders). Read for rhythm and technique, not topic. Each is followed by the specific moves that keep it from reading as generic copy.

---

## 1. A welcome note (storytelling mode)

> Over the years, through my work in research and development, product design, learning and development, creative work, and technology, I've come across a lot of knowledge that usually takes years to encounter properly. Some of it comes from research, some from working on real projects, and some from simply making enough mistakes to understand why something works the way it does.
>
> I started putting these things together because I wanted to make that knowledge easier to reach. Not by reducing everything into simple answers, but by bringing together the ideas, methods, references, and practical experience that can help someone understand a subject and eventually use it for themselves.
>
> That is what this library is becoming. A place where I can collect and organise the things I've learned, the things I'm still learning, and the things I think are worth passing on. Some of the material here may be useful to someone already working in the field. Some may be useful to someone who has never encountered the subject before.
>
> If something I've spent years learning can eventually help someone understand their first product idea, work through a hobby project, make sense of a research paper, or build something they otherwise would not have known how to build, then the effort is worth it.
>
> Maybe one day some of this will reach a kid working on a hobby project and give them a head start no one gave me.

**Moves:**
- Opens on the work and the problem, no "Welcome to..." and no mission statement.
- "some from simply making enough mistakes to understand why something works" - concrete, slightly self-deprecating, not "hard-won expertise".
- "That is what this library is becoming." - present continuous, honest that it's unfinished. Not "our library is".
- The "if X, then the effort is worth it" close names a specific small reader (a first product idea, a hobby project) rather than "learners everywhere".
- Final line reaches one step past the immediate point - a hope about who this eventually reaches, stated flat ("a head start no one gave me"), not "and that would be incredible". A reflective close like this is optional and should be genuinely the writer's; some writers code-switch into their first language for this one line.

## 2. What this is (storytelling / explaining)

> This library is where I collect the knowledge I've picked up through research, product design, learning and development, creative work, and technology, and try to put it in a form someone else can actually use.
>
> Information has never been the scarce part. There is more of it than anyone could get through. The harder thing is turning it into knowledge: something you can examine, connect to what you already know, place in context, and apply to a real problem in front of you.
>
> So this is less an archive than a workspace. It holds frameworks, research, references, learning materials, experiments, and working documents, some made first-hand and some gathered from elsewhere, kept in a way that invites you to take them apart and reuse them rather than only read them.
>
> I wanted it to stay open. Not sitting behind an institution that decides who gets to learn what, and not filtered through a feed built to hold attention. Just the material, organised well enough that a learner, an educator, a researcher, or someone building on their own can find a way through it.

**Moves:**
- "Information has never been the scarce part. There is more of it than anyone could get through." - two short declaratives before the complex sentence. Sets rhythm.
- "The harder thing is turning it into knowledge: something you can examine, connect..., place..., and apply..." - the colon replaces a "which means" clause; the list is verbs, not adjectives.
- "less an archive than a workspace" - defines by contrast in four words.
- "I wanted it to stay open." as its own sentence, then the two things it is *not* (an institution, a feed) before the one thing it is.
- No "we believe knowledge should be free". The value shows up as a design choice ("I wanted it to stay open"), not a creed.

## 3. Who this is for (explaining mode)

> Most of what I've put here sits at roughly an upper-intermediate level. Not expert, and not a first introduction either. I've assumed you can already hold the shape of an argument in your head, follow a line of reasoning without every step spelled out, and tell the difference between a claim and the evidence for it.
>
> You will hit terms you don't know. That is expected. It is not a sign the material isn't for you, and it isn't something I've tried to design out by watering the ideas down. The unfamiliar word is the doorway, not the wall.
>
> When you hit a term you don't understand, open a chat with an AI model and start asking. What does this mean. Give me an example. Now a simpler one. How does this connect to the thing I read a minute ago. Keep going until the idea is actually exposed to you and not just defined at you.
>
> Think of it as a conversation with something that has shelves and shelves of books loaded into it, patient enough to explain the same thing five different ways.

**Moves:**
- "Not expert, and not a first introduction either." - a fragment, placing the level by what it isn't.
- "That is expected." - three words, its own sentence, defuses the worry without a paragraph of reassurance.
- "The unfamiliar word is the doorway, not the wall." - the one aphoristic line. Parallel structure, no sentence after it explaining it.
- The questions are dropped in as bare fragments with full stops, several without question marks - it reads like actual typing, not a formatted list.
- "exposed to you and not just defined at you" - the whole point of the method, in six words.
- The analogy ("shelves and shelves of books... patient enough to explain the same thing five different ways") carries the explanation. Domestic and concrete, not "a knowledgeable digital assistant".

## 4. A bio (explaining mode, third person) - as a skeleton

> [Name] is a [role], [role], and [role] working across [two or three fields]. At [age], they founded [Project], an independent research lab exploring [two or three topics]. They also [one external marker of traction - a shortlist, a grant, a cohort].
>
> A former [prior role] and [prior role], [Name] brings experience across [two areas]. They contribute to the open-source community by [specific thing built], while teaching [specific subjects].
>
> Their work explores how emerging technology can extend what a person is able to do. Their aim is to make capable tools reach more people, and to leave the people who use them more able than it found them.

**Moves:**
- Plain appositive stacking: "a [role], [role], and [role] working across [fields]". No adjectives like "visionary" or "award-winning".
- "At [age], they founded..." - the fact carries the weight; no "impressively" or "already".
- Traction stated as one flat clause, not sold.
- The mission sentences stay declarative: "Their aim is to..." - never "They are deeply passionate about..." or "On a mission to fundamentally transform...".
- Even the largest claim ("extend what a person is able to do") is phrased as a plain description of the work, in a level voice.
- Fill the brackets with specific facts, not more adjectives. If a bracket can only be filled with a buzzword, cut the sentence.
