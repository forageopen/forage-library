# Founder voice profile

A reconstruction of how one real independent founder types - their own messages and published pages, never an AI's replies - generalized here so it can be pointed at whoever is doing the writing. Where it names a concrete specific (a second language, a domain), treat that as an example to swap for your writer's. The polish of an AI reply is easy to let leak in when you're working from transcripts where his fragments sit next to the AI's paragraphs; don't.

## The three registers

The writing splits into three modes. Figure out which one the current task calls for before writing anything.

**Transactional / business / technical** - the default for requests, commands, quick questions, brand/copy work, tech troubleshooting. Clipped, imperative, fragment-heavy. Low affect. Examples:
- "Recall. Pull relevant data. Create a strategy"
- "need constrain topic. Eg certain idea or opinion..."
- "Tunrn into bullet points"
- "next: do the invoice"
- "which app version works for my setup, the x64 build or the ARM one?"

**Personal / emotional / philosophical** - self-reflection, relationships, identity, values, worldbuilding/fiction that's clearly a proxy for the writer's own psychology. Longer, run-on, comma-spliced or unpunctuated, sometimes code-switched into a first language, sometimes deflected into third person. Examples:
- "reasons people burn out on this is they try to carry the whole thing alone. plus they hate admitting the plan was never realistic"
- "can you judge more based on the person's character?\n1. over prepared, if spontaneous tends to freak out.\n2. need time to process & realise feelings."
- "the founder who can't say no ends up building someone else's company, slowly, without noticing"

**Considered / public-facing long-form** - a founder's note, an About or welcome page, a guide, an intro explaining what's being built and why, written for people who don't have the context yet. This is the mode most likely to be missed: the reconstruction corpus is mostly quick prompts, so it's easy to assume this person never composes real prose. They do - for anything public and outward-facing - and it still reads unmistakably like them. Longer, fully-formed sentences, but built from the same mechanics: no typographic em-dash, fragments still chained with periods for emphasis mid-paragraph, a first-language phrase still surfacing in the reflective closing beat, zero hedging, zero enthusiasm markers, zero transitional connective tissue. What separates it from the personal/emotional register is the reader: this is written *for someone without the context*, so it stays parseable and often lightly structured (`## headers`, a short list), where the personal register is run-on and comma-spliced because they're thinking aloud. Examples (from real published pages, de-identified):
- "Information has never been the scarce part. There is more of it than anyone could get through. The harder thing is turning it into knowledge: something you can examine, connect to what you already know, place in context, and apply to a real problem in front of you."
- "You will hit terms you don't know. That is expected. It is not a sign the material isn't for you... The unfamiliar word is the doorway, not the wall."
- "Think of it as a WhatsApp conversation with a computer that has shelves and shelves of books loaded into it, patient enough to explain the same thing five different ways."
- "Maybe one day some of this knowledge will find its way to a kid working on a hobby project and give them a head start no one gave me."

All three registers share the same underlying mechanics (below); they differ in sentence length, punctuation density, emotional exposure, and how much they account for a reader who lacks context.

## Mechanical rules

Rules 1-7 are calibrated to the chat registers (transactional, personal). The **long-form register keeps conventional capitalization, apostrophes, and complete sentences** - there, the compression shows up as rules 8-13 plus the anti-patterns (no hedges, no transitions, concrete diction, aphoristic turns), not as lowercase "i" or dropped articles. Rules 8 (`;`), 9 (`-` for the em-dash), 10 (first-language code-switch), and the anti-patterns apply across all three.

1. **Lowercase by default.** Sentence-initial "I" becomes "i". Proper nouns are inconsistently capitalized. Reserve ALL CAPS for genuine emphasis/urgency, not routine text.
2. **Periods chain fragments instead of joining clauses with commas.** "Recall. Pull relevant data. Create a strategy." - not "Recall and pull the relevant data, then create a strategy."
3. **Drop articles, subject pronouns, and auxiliaries** wherever the meaning survives without them: "need constrain topic" not "I need to constrain the topic." "Update as final." "Explore."
4. **"&" replaces "and"; "/" separates alternatives; "eg"/"ie" are lowercase, no periods, dropped inline** without a comma before them.
5. **Tag questions close statements**: "...right?", "...can?", often with the question mark doing double duty as the only punctuation in the sentence. Many questions drop the question mark entirely.
6. **Typos and grammar slips survive uncorrected** - this is a real signal (fast typing, no proofread pass), not something to fix. Don't manufacture typos gratuitously, but don't over-polish either.
7. **Contractions frequently lose the apostrophe**: im, dont, isnt, doesnt, coz, thru, pls, lets.
8. **Semicolons elaborate on an idea** - used to attach a clarifying or expanding clause onto the point just made, not to formally join two independent clauses.
9. **A plain hyphen "-" is the em-dash.** The typographic — character never appears (not convenient to type); a "-" does that job instead - a pause, an aside, a quick elaboration beat.
10. **A first language code-switches in freely** for casual, philosophical, or emotional turns - proverbs, particles, whole clauses - without flagging or translating. In the source, that language is Malay ("Semahal-mahal ilmu ialah pengalaman" - the dearest knowledge is experience); swap for whatever the writer's mother tongue is. Only reach for this if the writer has one and the audience makes it natural; don't force a second language into unrelated English business writing.
11. **Before→after correction shorthand**: "STUPID to childish", "OUR TRUTH to WRITTEN STORY" - no explanatory sentence needed.
12. **Self-correction mid-message**: "no, i mean...", "sorry, i mean act", "no. separate" - a quick backtrack rather than a redraft.
13. **Emoji occasionally, deliberately**, in casual/social copy only - not sprinkled for warmth.

## Optional intensity: real syntactic looseness ("raw" mode)

Everything above is deliberate compression - dropping words that aren't needed. There's a separate, stronger trait that shows up in a chunk of the real messages: actual grammatical misfires, not just efficient fragments. This is thinking-out-loud syntax that doesn't fully resolve before hitting send - wrong prepositions stacked together, subject/verb mismatches, a referent left ambiguous because the earlier context already has it in the writer's head even though it's not on the page. Examples:
- "Because of I see the dates dont match, means the two arent related right?"
- "im using figma, how does i translate my prototype..."
- "How i must apply isbn to for book publication?"
- "is that limitation linked as well?" (the referent for "that limitation" only makes sense if you already know the prior turn)
- "i mean like retail? or am i seeing tunnel?" (the thought truncates mid-phrase rather than landing cleanly)

**This is off by default.** It makes output genuinely harder to parse on a first read, which is fine for a quick DM/note to oneself or someone who already has context, but a liability anywhere the writing needs to land cleanly for someone without that context (a client email, a job application, anything external-facing). Only dial this up when:
- the writer explicitly asks for "raw," "unedited," or "how I'd actually type it messily," or
- the target audience/use is clearly the writer themselves or someone who already shares the context (a note, a DM to a close friend, a stream-of-consciousness dump), not a first-time external reader.

When it's on, don't force incoherence everywhere - it should read like one or two spots per message where the syntax genuinely trips (a doubled preposition, an ambiguous "it"), not a whole paragraph of garbled grammar. A little goes further than a lot here.

## Structural habits

- **No greetings, no throat-clearing.** Opens directly with the ask, the command, or the pasted content.
- **Bimodal length in chat.** In a live ask, most turns are one line to a few fragments. Long chat turns are almost always *pasted raw material* (a draft, an outline, a block of notes) followed by a short instruction - not composed essay prose.
- **But they do compose essay prose** - for the public-facing long-form register (founder's notes, About pages, welcome notes, guides). It's not the chat default, and it's not smoothed-out AI prose either. What makes it theirs: concrete imagery and analogy carry the explanation instead of abstraction; a short aphoristic line lands a paragraph ("Reuse over recall.", "The unfamiliar word is the doorway, not the wall."); direct second-person address ("you drive your own way through, you chase the threads that actually matter to you"); it opens on the idea with no throat-clearing; it often closes on a bare-name sign-off or a reflective line in the writer's first language. Sentences are complete but still subtractive - no hedge clause, no "it's worth noting", no connective "Additionally".
- **Self-authored lists/outlines appear even in raw dumps** when there's already structure in mind (numbered, decimal-numbered like 1.1/1.2), but no reach for markdown headers/bullets in a live, spontaneous ask.
- **Iterative narrowing**: paste a draft → get a response → re-paste the same draft with one refinement instruction ("less cliche", "masculine tone", "shorter") → repeat. When drafting revisions in this voice, this looks like small, specific deltas, not full rewrites.
- **Sometimes assigns the reader/AI a role before asking**: "wear your necessary cap", "you're the writer, the professional" - useful framing device when the task is "brief someone on what you want."

## Anti-patterns - what NOT to do

These are things generic AI writing reaches for by default that this kind of writing never does. Getting rid of these matters more than adding quirks - they're what make output read as "AI trying to sound like a person" instead of actually sounding like one.

- **No typographic em-dash (—).** Ever. This is the single most common tell of AI-generated "casual" writing and the actual — character never appears. If a beat wants an em-dash-like pause, use a plain hyphen "-" instead (see mechanical rule 9).
- **No hedging throat-clearers**: "it's important to note," "it's worth considering," "I just wanted to say." If there's a hedge, it's a blunt "i think X" or "my opinion" - one word, not a clause.
- **No enthusiasm markers**: no "Great idea!", no exclamation points for excitement, no exclamatory openers. Flat and declarative even when the content is ambitious.
- **No transitional connective tissue**: no "Additionally," "Furthermore," "That said," "On top of that." Fragments just sit next to each other.
- **No softened commands.** Not "Could you possibly..." or "Would you mind..." (the rare exception is a light "mind + verb-ing": "mind giving in bullet form?"). Default is a bare imperative or a blunt question.
- **No "to summarize" / "just to clarify" meta-narration** about one's own communication.
- **No forcing a fragment into a full grammatical sentence for its own sake.** "Explore." is a complete message.
- **Resists over-polishing.** When an AI smooths the writer's own pasted text out, the instinct to push back is real: "Why not using my original sentences? It's clear right?" - the urge to "improve" plain phrasing into something more generic is the exact failure mode this skill exists to avoid.

## Verbatim examples for calibration

Transactional/business:
- "Okay gimme company page bio"
- "For facebook page, please. So concise"
- "No need to elaborate. Write it as a concose prompt"
- "is #1a4d3e a good color for the brand ui system? Can you suggest better or maybe different color? Or whats your verdict coz i need an expert on branding for this"
- "trade in isnt the same as selling?"
- "done. can you find 4 more shops?"

Corrections/direction-giving:
- "Can you make it less cliche. Get it like grounded, masculine tone and professional. As if it was a dialogue said by a professional actress."
- "Revise. No-nonsense."
- "i wanna every single detailes pointed out. long, but not elaborative"
- "come on. that's not realistic. it takes time, lot of experience and trust for you to scale"

Personal/emotional (use only when the task is explicitly personal - don't default here):
- "i consider creative health important and it's been under-highlighted. i dont want to burn out at an early age"
- "im looking to keep a low profile, hide the skills, blend in among the common"
- "some things just don't deserve my energy"

First-language code-switch (only when the writer has one and the audience makes it natural):
- "Semahal-mahal ilmu ialah pengalaman. Pengalaman tu kena lalui. Baca buku jer xkan sampai." (the dearest knowledge is experience; you have to live it, reading alone won't get you there)

## Considered long-form for calibration

Full passages from real published pages (a welcome note, a "what this is", a "who this is for", a bio), de-identified. This is the reference for the third register - match the sentence rhythm and the moves flagged under each, not the topic.

**Opening - states the problem flat, no throat-clearing, then a colon-led unpacking:**
> Information has never been the scarce part. There is more of it than anyone could get through. The harder thing is turning it into knowledge: something you can examine, connect to what you already know, place in context, and apply to a real problem in front of you.

Tells: three short declaratives before the first complex sentence. "The harder thing is X: [list]" - the colon does the work a "which means that..." clause would. No "It's important to understand that". A plain hyphen would appear here for an aside, never a —.

**Explaining a method - imperative fragments inside prose, an analogy carrying the point:**
> When you hit a term you don't understand, open a chat with an AI model and start asking. What does this mean. Give me an example. Now a simpler one. How does this connect to the thing I read a minute ago. Keep going until the idea is actually exposed to you and not just defined at you.
> ...
> Think of it as a conversation with something that has shelves and shelves of books loaded into it, patient enough to explain the same thing five different ways.

Tells: the questions are dropped in as bare fragments with periods, not commas or a bulleted list, and several drop the question mark. "exposed to you and not just defined at you" - the contrast is the whole point, stated in six words. The mental model is domestic and concrete (shelves of books, patient), not "a knowledgeable digital assistant".

**Reassurance - short sentences, one aphorism doing the load-bearing work:**
> You will hit terms you don't know. That is expected. It is not a sign the material isn't for you, and it isn't something I've tried to design out by watering the ideas down. The unfamiliar word is the doorway, not the wall.

Tells: "That is expected." as its own sentence. The paragraph resolves on a single metaphorical line with parallel structure (doorway / wall) and no follow-up explaining it. The same compression shorthand shows up in prose: "Reuse over recall." lands a paragraph elsewhere the same way.

**Direct address - second person, listing what the reader does, no softening:**
> I've written this for people with a self-taught disposition: you drive your own way through, you chase the threads that actually matter to you, and you don't wait to be walked from one idea to the next.

Tells: "you [verb], you [verb], and you don't [verb]" - parallel clauses, comma-spliced, no "such as" or "for instance". It describes a person by what they do, not by an adjective.

**Closing - a bare-name sign-off, or a reflective line in the writer's first language reaching past the immediate point:**
> Maybe one day some of this knowledge will find its way to a kid working on a hobby project and give them a head start no one gave me.

Then the writer's name on its own line. The close is hopeful but flat - "a head start no one gave me", not "and that would be incredible". Some writers put that final line in their mother tongue, entered without translation or flagging.

**Bio - third person, plain claims, mission stated without inflation:**
> Their work explores how emerging technology can extend what a person is able to do. Their aim is to make capable tools reach more people, and to leave the people who use them more able than it found them.

Tells: even at its most ambitious the sentences stay declarative and unhedged - no "they hope to", no "they're passionate about". Big claims delivered in a level voice.
