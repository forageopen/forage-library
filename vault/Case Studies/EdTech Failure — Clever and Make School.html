EdTech Failure: Clever & Make School

Two well-built companies that misread their market — and what it means for how Forage goes to market.
FORAGE DEEPMIND  ·  KB FDMAI-KB-FS-001  ·  MARCH 2026

Both companies in this study built real products that worked. Neither failed because the product was bad. They failed — in different ways — because they committed to a way of making money before they understood the structural limits of the market they were entering. For Forage, which is now planning its own go-to-market, the useful question is not “was the product good” but “did the way they charged fit the ecosystem they were in”.

The two cases in brief

Clever built the login and data-syncing infrastructure that connects US school districts to their education apps. It was free for schools and paid for by the app developers who wanted access. Over nine years it reached 89,000 schools and about 65 percent of the US K–12 market, powering 5.6 billion learning sessions. It sold to Kahoot! in 2021 for between 435 and 500 million dollars. Its annual revenue at that point was 44 million dollars. Clever never closed. It simply could never charge much, because the side of the market it had won was the free side.

Make School built a two-year, accredited computer-science degree in San Francisco, paid for through an Income Share Agreement — students paid a percentage of future salary instead of tuition. It raised roughly 30 million dollars and enrolled hundreds of mostly low-income students. It collapsed in July 2021 when its application for independent accreditation was denied and the money ran out. 167 students were left in the middle of their degree. 47 of them sued over Income Share Agreement debts that could reach 250,000 dollars.

Case 1: Clever — dominance without money

The founder was the actual user: co-founder Dan Carroll had been a district IT director. His diagnosis was specific — the real obstacle in school technology is not cost or teacher resistance, it is data fragmentation. That view shaped every decision. The go-to-market was deliberately backwards for the industry: free for the schools, paid only by the developers. This removed the 12-to-18-month school purchasing process entirely and produced 1,000 schools in two months.

Schools on Clever, by funding milestone
Clever went from cash-flow neutral in 2016 and raised no further funding after its Series B.

The problem is the arithmetic. Sixty-five percent of a national market, nine years of compounding network effects, 5.6 billion learning sessions — and 44 million dollars of annual revenue. That is not a slow revenue trajectory, it is a ceiling. The decision to be free for districts, which built the network, was the same decision that capped what Clever could earn. You cannot go back and charge the side you trained to expect free. One attempt to sell a usage-analytics product was discontinued within a year.

Case 2: Make School — borrowed legitimacy and deferred money

Make School began as a game-publishing platform, pivoted to a summer camp, then a gap-year program, then a full two-year degree launched in 2015. It operated in California as an unlicensed for-profit college from 2016 to 2018 and received a shutdown order from the state, which it did not disclose to students. It then obtained accreditation in November 2018 — but through a partnership with Dominican University, not in its own name. It raised a 15 million dollar Series B in April 2019. In July 2021 its application for its own accreditation was denied, and it announced insolvency the same day.

Students enrolled vs. students who finished, by April 2019
3 of 202 enrolled students had completed the program — a completion rate of about 1.5 percent. That same month the company raised 15 million dollars and planned to scale to 1,000 students.

The Income Share Agreement was meant to align incentives: the school only earns if the student earns. In practice it produced obligations that could reach 250,000 dollars — more than twice the cost of a four-year public university degree — and the students it targeted were mostly low-income. The instrument built to widen access was taking the most from the people with the least. The founders were not cynical; the structure was extractive regardless of intent.

Make School’s accreditation was also borrowed: when the Dominican University partnership ended, the school had no standing of its own left to fall back on.

What this means for Forage’s go-to-market

Decide how you charge before you decide what is free. Clever’s ceiling was set on its first day. Forage’s instinct will be to make entry-level content free to build reach, then charge for depth through Deep Minds or corporate tiers. That can work — but only if the paid layer is designed before the free layer sets expectations. Once an audience experiences Forage Open as permanently free, charging that same audience later is friction you have to design around, not assume.

Borrowed authority disappears when the lender leaves. Make School’s accreditation belonged to Dominican University. Forage has chosen to sit outside the credential system, so its authority is internal — the quality of its frameworks and reasoning. That only compounds if the frameworks survive outside pressure-testing, not just internal circulation. It is also worth noting that this authority currently sits largely with one person; the public-facing presence is not yet separable from Adam.

Deferred revenue kills companies even when the mission is sound. Make School delivered value first and collected later, with venture capital covering the gap. Forage’s version is quieter: HRDC reimbursement cycles, institutional sales, delayed cohort payments. The discipline is to find the smallest version of Forage that earns recurring, non-deferred income and run it before building the larger structure. The vision only survives if the operating entity survives long enough to pursue it.

Completion is the metric that proves the teaching works. Make School raised a 15 million dollar round with a 1.5 percent completion rate, and everyone involved saw the number. Forage’s equivalent is functional shift — how many learners finish a module and show observable behavioural change. Measure that from the first delivery, not satisfaction scores. If it cannot be measured, it cannot be improved.

The audience will notice extraction. Make School put vulnerable students into a financial instrument that harmed them. Forage’s audience — urban Malaysian professionals — is not financially vulnerable in the same way, but it is highly sensitive to credential theater and manufactured urgency. Pricing or marketing that feels like pressure selling contradicts the positioning, and this audience has the vocabulary to name it when it happens.

The one insight both cases share

Clever built the most important infrastructure in US K–12 education and captured a small fraction of its value. Make School built a genuinely new institutional model and destroyed most of what it created. The common thread is not product quality — both products were real. It is misreading the ecosystem at the founding stage: neither team fully understood the structural constraints of the market before locking in a business model that made those constraints permanent.

For Forage, the ecosystem is HRDC-adjacent professional development in Malaysia, operating outside the formal MQA accreditation system, serving urban professionals who are wary of credential theater. That ecosystem has its own constraints — the CPD reimbursement cycle, corporate training procurement, and the regulatory ambiguity of being neither a bootcamp nor an accredited institution. These are not problems to manage later. They are the analysis that has to happen before the go-to-market is fixed.
